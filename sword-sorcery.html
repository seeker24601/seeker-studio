01

Why

I played D&D growing up, and started playing AI Dungeon in 2018. When language models learned to call tools, I got the idea for a game master that enforces the rules through them: the model tells the story and the engine decides what happens.

Other games have since tried the same idea. Some follow simplified rulesets of their own, but the AI still improvises almost everything.

AI is great at narrating a scene, voicing characters and reacting to whatever you try, but often falls short on original ideas and on planning a story across many sessions. So a person writes each campaign, the AI runs it within the author's guidelines, and code enforces the rules. That plays better than a story the model invents as it goes, or one limited to what the author could predict.

02

Making it

I designed and built the game end to end: the rules engine, the agentic engineering pipeline, the world, the campaigns and the art direction. AI wrote most of the code from standards I set.

Agentic engineering

The game is a set of modular systems, such as combat, memory and campaigns, that interact with each other. Building it in modules isolates the blast radius of a bug. AI agents handle a change inside one system well, but often miss how it affects the others.

The code is built using a custom-made harness. Each change has to name the systems it touches and link their documentation, an independent agent reviews it, and the repository refuses any push to production except the exact commit approved for release.

Models

Different models handle different work. The main model narrates and calls the engine’s tools, a separate model narrates combat, and a cheaper auxiliary model works in the background after each turn, tracking the scene, suggesting actions and updating the world’s memory. If a model fails, a fallback takes over.

World and rules

The world is original, but inspired by settings like Greyhawk, because I prefer grounded, old-school settings with humans in focus. Also, Wizards of the Coast owns the Forgotten Realms, which is what comes to mind when most people hear "D&D". The rules are 5e, because the SRD is openly licensed and many players are familiar with them through Baldur's Gate 3.

Visual direction

The art is generated through Midjourney in a painted style, and the interface borrows from pixel art, as a throwback to the era that inspired the game. I wanted it to feel nostalgic, with dark fantasy elements that stop short of grimdark. The sound design aims for the same balance.

03

Trade-offs

Combat runs on zones

Problem
Tracking 5e movement foot by foot would have needed a grid and a map view, which would have slowed combat down and made it less fun.
Choice
Combatants stand in zones about 30 feet apart. You advance, retreat or flee one zone at a time, and the engine checks weapon and spell ranges against the gap.
Cost
Positions are approximate, so a 10-foot reach weapon still needs its target in the same zone, just like a sword.

Decision models read intent

Problem
Matching player input with keywords and regex turned into whack-a-mole. Each new pattern missed the next phrasing, and slightly different wording could confuse or undermine the engine.
Choice
A decision model picks from options the engine lists, including "unclear", and the engine acts only on a clear pick. Each change to a decision model has to pass a fixed set of test situations first, and two candidates that failed them never shipped.
Cost
A model call costs money and adds latency where a regex was free and instant, so calls are kept to the decisions that need one.

Memory is layered

Problem
Long campaigns outgrew the prompt, and no model could hold everything that happened.
Choice
Recent turns stay word for word, older ones are compressed into summaries, and what no longer matters is pushed out gradually. Each agent, such as a companion, keeps its own memory of what it learned.
Cost
Compression can add several seconds to a turn. Memory also has to account for everything a character may remember from one campaign to the next, including events that may or may not have happened.
04

In play

You play an authored campaign or scenario, with heroes you create or pick and AI companions in your party. The world responds to everything you do, and the game master voices everyone you meet.

Try anything

Your hero can say or do anything within the rules, in your own words. The game carries out what the rules allow and declines what they don’t.

Choices stick

What you do is remembered, from how people feel about you to what changed in the places you’ve been. Companions keep their own memories, and your hero carries their level, gear and history into the next campaign.

Fast, narrated combat

Fights move quickly and still follow the 5e rules. Each round is narrated, so combat reads as part of the story rather than an action game.

05

So far

Sword & Sorcery is a playable alpha, free with the included model or with your own key. Multiplayer may come later.

What I learned

Put a rule in code before you put it in a prompt.

Code runs every time. A prompt only works if the model remembers to call the right tool, and sometimes it doesn’t.

Give each fact one owner.

The worst bugs come from two systems deciding the same outcome.

Rules exist to make the game fun.

Measuring exact distances and tracking ammunition and carrying weight turned out to be less fun in play than I imagined, so I simplified them.
